Reading sheet music on a Windows 2-in-1

Most advice about reading music from a tablet assumes an iPad. A lot of musicians already own a Windows laptop that folds into a tablet, and it works well on a music stand once it is set up for it.

Why a 2-in-1 works on a stand

A 2-in-1 has a hinge that folds all the way back, so the keyboard sits behind the screen. That leaves a flat touchscreen facing you, with a pen for markings. Windows switches to tablet mode on its own when the keyboard is folded away.

There are two ways to set one up:

  • Tablet mode. Fully folded flat and resting on the stand's ledge like a folder of music.
  • Tent mode. Folded into an upside-down V and standing on a table or a flat stand. Useful for a keyboard rack or a table at a rehearsal.

Screen size and shape

Screen size gets all the attention, but the shape of the screen matters as much. A printed letter-size page is about 1.3 times as tall as it is wide. The closer a screen's shape is to that, the less space goes to waste.

I own two 2-in-1s with different shapes, which makes the difference easy to see:

A few things stand out:

  • Bigger screens win when upright. The 15.6-inch Spectre shows a part at nearly printed size. It is also a lot of laptop to balance on a stand.
  • Two pages sideways means smaller notes. Around two thirds of printed size is readable for clean, well-engraved parts. It gets harder with dense or handwritten charts.
  • A 13-inch 2-in-1 is close to a 13-inch Surface. You do not need a Surface to get a good result.

Standly switches between one page and two-page view based on the window width, so rotating the screen is all it takes. Many PDFs have wide white margins. Framing in Standly crops them away and makes the notes bigger. The calculator shows how much that helps.

Settings to change before a gig

Check these before your first rehearsal with it. A screen that goes dark or a notification that pops up in the middle of a tune is hard to fix while you are playing.

In Windows

  • Rotation lock. Lock the screen in the orientation you want. Otherwise, bumping the stand can flip the page.
  • Do not disturb. Turn it on so notifications do not appear over the music.
  • Windows Update active hours. Set them to cover your rehearsal times, so the laptop never restarts to install updates mid-gig.
  • Battery saver. Decide whether it should turn on automatically. It dims the screen, which matters on a dark stage.

In Standly

  • Keep screen on in Settings → Viewer stops the display from dimming or sleeping while music is open. It replaces changing the Windows sleep settings.
  • Full screen hides the toolbar and leaves only the music.
  • Invert document colors shows white notes on black, which cuts glare on a dark stage.
  • Half-page navigation turns half a page at a time, so the line you are reading stays on screen while the next one appears.

Marking up your music

Most 2-in-1s support a pen, sometimes sold separately. Standly records pen pressure, so strokes get thicker when you press harder. You can also mark with a finger. Your annotations stay with the piece, even if you move or rename the file later.

Turning pages

Tapping the screen works, but it takes a hand off your instrument. A Bluetooth pedal or button turns pages without that. I use a cheap Bluetooth media remote. The page turner guide covers the options and how to set them up.

Other devices that work

Standly runs on any Windows 10 or 11 device, including laptops and desktops. For reading music on a stand, the most common choices are:

  • Surface Pro. A tablet with a detachable keyboard, currently sold in 12-inch and 13-inch sizes. Both have a 3:2 screen, which is a close fit for upright pages.
  • Surface Go. No longer in Microsoft's lineup, but common secondhand. It is smaller and lighter, and its 10.5-inch screen shows a letter page at about two thirds of printed size.
  • Other 2-in-1s, such as HP's Envy x360 and Spectre x360 or Lenovo's Yoga models. Check the screen size and shape in the calculator before buying.
  • A regular laptop on a keyboard's music rack, for pianists and organists.
